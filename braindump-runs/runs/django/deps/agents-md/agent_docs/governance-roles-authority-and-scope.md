# Governance Roles, Authority, and Scope

> Defining governance bodies, roles, responsibilities, approval authority, escalation paths, Code of Conduct scope, and role lifecycle.

**When to check**: When writing or editing governance/process documentation that defines roles, authority, or oversight.

## Rules

<!-- rule:58 -->
- Name the responsible actor in every normative statement in governance docs (`DEPs`, `draft/`, `accepted/`, `final/*.rst`) — unnamed obligations can't be enforced or audited. — These rules apply across all governance and process docs (`draft/`, `accepted/`, `final/`). Each bullet ties the requirement to a concrete failure mode: obligations with no owner can't be enforced, references to non-existent bodies (informal WGs, "core contributors") leave decisions unowned, and unscoped or passive phrasing lets governance powers or requirements drift beyond intent.
  - Attribute obligations, powers, and adjudication only to formally-constituted bodies (`Steering Council`, `Board`, `Fellows`, `Security Team`), never informal groups, generic labels like "core contributors", or actions any contributor can perform — those have no authority to act on.
  - Name the body that adjudicates whether breach, remediation, or deadline requirements were satisfied before the process advances — an unassigned gate stalls the process with no authority to move it.
  - Specify the scope and limits of any granted permission or right — unscoped powers get interpreted beyond intent.
  - Avoid passive constructions and undefined role references, and keep grammar correct (e.g., subject-verb agreement for singular roles) — ambiguity in who acts invites disputes.
<!-- rule:63 -->
- Reference governance bodies, teams, and roles in Django DEPs and DSF/Django process docs by their canonical names with exact capitalization — stale or generic terms (e.g. `Django Infrastructure Team`, `Technical Board`, `committee` in CoC contexts, bare `board members`) cause readers and tooling to treat one body as several. — When names drift across `final/`, `draft/`, and `accepted/` docs, links break, role authority becomes ambiguous, and readers can't tell whether `Django Ops team` and `Django Infrastructure Team` are the same body. Centralizing the review/approval role term in one place lets the underlying body change without rewriting every DEP.
<!-- rule:187 -->
- **Eligibility & activity:** Don't gate team/board/Triage & Review Team eligibility on recency or measurable activity thresholds — they silently exclude non-code contributors and invite arbitrary cutoffs. — 
<!-- rule:74 -->
- In process/governance docs like DEPs, define roles by concrete responsibilities, required judgment/skill, and practical necessity using measurable criteria. Lead with qualifiers (required/optional, scope) before detailing duties. Keep each role section scoped — no unrelated topics, disparaging or bureaucratic framing, or hedging language that understates the role. Clear eligibility criteria prevent disputes about who qualifies and keep role participation unambiguous. — Role sections function as eligibility criteria; vague or hedged definitions invite disputes about who qualifies, while off-topic or disparaging framing undermines the role's legitimacy in governance proposals.
<!-- rule:75 -->
- Define governance body scope, powers, decision mechanism, and accountability explicitly. — Governance and DEP docs span multiple DEP directories (`draft/`, `accepted/`, `final/`), so consistency depends on each section standing alone; unclear scope or special-case procedures cause divergent interpretations when AI assistants edit role definitions.
  Governance docs must state the body's scope and powers, how decisions are initiated and deadlocks resolved, and how members are appointed externally — undefined process leaves AI edits guessing at authority boundaries.
  Make each Django governance DEP role section self-contained.
  In every role section, cover responsibilities, decision rights, permitted/forbidden role combinations, and appointment/removal rules — readers shouldn't need to cross-reference other sections to understand a role.
  Give a rationale for every role restriction.
  State why a role may or may not overlap with another — restrictions without reasons get silently removed in later edits.
  Avoid special-case role-overlap procedures in removal/voting sections.
  Route all overlap handling through the general mechanism — one-off procedures in `remove`/`vote` text diverge from the base rules and hide inconsistencies.
  Repeat minimum roster size in selection and roster-maintenance sections.
  Restate any minimum roster requirement where selection or roster changes happen — a single mention elsewhere is easy to miss and lets edits break quorum assumptions.
<!-- rule:125 -->
- Define approval authority and escalation in governance docs — undefined 'in accordance with' stalls decisions; in Django DEPs/RFCs/release governance, require Steering Council for experimental features, RFC 2119 `MAY`/`SHALL` for cross-team flows, and emergency authorization (e.g., non-objection from one Board member) so urgent fixes aren't blocked by full votes. — Ambiguous phrases and undefined proposal categories leave decisions blocked; explicit approvers, RFC 2119 keywords, and a lightweight emergency path keep governance actionable.
<!-- rule:179 -->
- In governance/DEP docs, tie long-lived privileged roles (`Mergers`, maintainers) to periodic self-renewal or rotation — activity expectations, term limits/automatic retirement, or roster review — document exceptions for funded positions, and keep low-privilege contributor roles lightweight. Privilege-proportional lifecycle requirements prevent entrenched roles while avoiding process friction on contributors. — Privileged roles without renewal mechanisms tend to entrench; heavy governance on low-privilege contributors adds friction without benefit. `draft/XXXX-dissolve-core.rst` and `accepted/0010-new-governance.rst` show the pattern this rule formalizes.
<!-- rule:210 -->
- Use only `low`/`medium`/`high` for security severities in docs — undefined terms like "critical" imply uncalibrated triage criteria — The project classifies security severity as `low`, `medium`, and `high` only. Adding qualitative qualifiers such as "critical" that have no documented criteria creates ambiguity: readers can't map the label to a response level, and different authors invent inconsistent thresholds. Because this documentation lives in varied places (e.g. `draft/` governance docs) rather than one docs directory, the convention must hold repo-wide.
<!-- rule:189 -->
- In governance/process proposals (e.g. `DEP` docs) that change roles, motivate as removing arbitrary prestige and aligning roles with work actually performed — not as bureaucratizing; separate formal role/permission changes from erasing public historical evidence/recognition; and name other contribution modes that remain necessary and valued — mischaracterizing intent or implying erasure triggers contributor pushback and stalls the proposal. — Careful, accurate wording around governance or process proposals that change roles prevents demeaning role descriptions, overclaimed impact, and the implication that de-emphasized roles are unimportant, which otherwise lead contributors to read formal role or permission changes as erasure of public historical evidence or recognition and to overlook contribution modes that remain necessary and valued. The concrete cost is avoidable conflict and lost trust: people are surprised by what actually changed, history and recognition are misread, and proposals become harder to evaluate and maintain.
<!-- rule:71 -->
- Define the Code of Conduct's scope in governance docs — name the responsible body (e.g., the CoC working group), state it applies to all governance roles (`Board`, `Steering Council`, `Security Team`), and specify what authority it does/does not have — prevents readers from extending the CoC to procedural, technical, or integrity disputes it doesn't cover — Without an explicit scope, readers may infer CoC authority over procedural/technical disputes or miss that it binds all governance bodies
<!-- rule:72 -->
- **In governance docs (e.g., DEP Rationales under `final/`), when relaxing oversight on elected bodies, list the safeguards that remain** (elections, Board/executive approval, CoC enforcement, no-confidence/removal votes, reversibility by the incoming body) and explain why the change is safe — framing it as enabling community operation and presuming positive intent. This prevents reviewers from reading the relaxation as ignoring abuse risk. — Governance relaxations (e.g., allowing actions during an election or transition period) invite abuse concerns; naming the surviving checks and the safety argument keeps the rationale defensible without requiring deontological language.
